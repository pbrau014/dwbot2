A map-first playbook for understanding how destroyers, cruisers, and battleships use terrain, objectives, support, and movement to shape a battle.

The aim is not to guess every ship's exact path. It is to identify the options the map creates, the choices each team is likely to make, and the responses that preserve or improve a team's position.

Core proposition

Position creates options.

A useful position lets a team see, threaten, support, contest, disengage, or rotate. A weak position removes options or leaves a ship dependent on help that cannot arrive.

  • Terrain shapes which routes and firing angles exist.
  • Ship class shapes the role a ship can commonly perform.
  • Support links determine whether a forward position is sustainable.
  • Objectives and score determine whether holding or moving is valuable.
  • New information changes the prediction; the model must update.

General tendency

What open water changes

  • Firing lines are easier to establish across distance when terrain does not block them.
  • Ships can maneuver across a broader area, but exposure may increase.
  • Crossfires become possible when friendly groups occupy separated angles.
  • A ship moving forward without support may become visible and vulnerable from several directions.
Do not equate “open” with “safe.” Open water can increase freedom of movement and increase the number of enemies able to engage.

  1. Does it block vision, fire, or both?
    These are separate questions.
  2. Which side has the safer exit?

A channel is not automatically the route to take. Compare the objective value with exposure, support availability, and the cost of turning or retreating.

10 · Position value

Score a position by its options—not by its apparent safety.

A position can be well protected but strategically useless. Another can be exposed yet valuable because it spots, threatens, supports a capture, and has a planned exit.

Value created

What the position enables

  • Objective pressure or denial.
  • Useful spotting and information.
  • Friendly fire support or a crossfire angle.
  • Control over a passage or approach.
  • Reinforcement and rotation opportunities.
  • A viable withdrawal route.
Cost incurred

What the position risks

  • Exposure to several enemy angles.
  • Loss of support because land blocks the line of fire.
  • Being trapped against an island or map edge.
  • Being isolated if the nearby group retreats.
  • Giving up another objective or lane.
  • Arriving too late to influence the engagement.
Compact mental test
Can I influence?

A target, area, route, or enemy decision.

Can I be supported?

A real friendly line of help, not just a nearby icon.

Can I leave?

A route that remains usable if the situation changes.

Why a team may choose it

Key risk
  • The lightly covered side may concede objectives or allow an enemy flank.
  • Concentrated ships can become vulnerable to a well-timed crossfire.
  • The force must know when to stop advancing and rotate.

21 · Prediction discipline

Never mix what is seen with what is inferred.

Observed

What is confirmed

Visible ship position, visible movement, confirmed capture state, visible islands, and the ship's last known location.

Inferred

24 · Confidence and evidence

Confidence should reflect the evidence—not the attractiveness of the story.

A movement can fit several intentions. Increase confidence only when independent observations support the same explanation and competing explanations become less plausible. State a plausible alternative and what new evidence would change your view.

High confidence

Several signs agree

Multiple independent observations support one interpretation, and the map makes the main alternatives difficult or less likely.

Example: a group advances together, its supporting ships follow, and its previous objective pressure falls.

Medium confidence

The pattern fits, but alternatives remain

The movement and terrain support an explanation, but one or more other intentions could produce the same visible pattern.

Keep the alternative visible and avoid an irreversible commitment.

Low confidence

Evidence is sparse or stale

The ship was seen only briefly, its last-known position is old, or several routes and intentions remain plausible.

Preserve options and seek another observation.

27 · Objectives

Territory matters because the game mode assigns it value.

Before predicting where ships will move, identify what the battle asks the team to achieve. The same patch of water can be strategically important in one mode and secondary in another.

Domination-style objectives

Multiple control areas

Ships must balance capture, contesting, denying points, and keeping enough force available to fight for the areas that matter most.

Ask: which area generates the strongest scoring pressure, and what is the minimum force needed to contest it?

Base / standard-style objectives

Protect or capture a base

Movement must account for the defended base, the enemy's route toward it, and the possibility of a win through capture rather than ship elimination.

Ask: which ships can intercept the route without abandoning the team's own base?

Central-zone / ring-style objectives

Pressure toward the center

The geometry can make central access, layered control, and the ability to reinforce the middle particularly important.

Ask: what does the mode reward, and what is the risk of entering the center too early?

Rules and scoring can vary by mode, battle type, and update. Confirm the current in-game objective and score rules before assigning exact point values or capture times. See the game modes reference.
28 · Battle progression

Each phase asks a different question.

Opening · deployment

Which routes are being used? Which objectives are being tested? Is the enemy concentrating or spreading out?

First contact · discovery

Where are the enemy groups? Which support links are real? Which initial assumptions were wrong?

Midgame · conversion

Can a local advantage become a capture, a retreat, an isolated target, or a safer rotation?

Endgame · win condition

What is needed now: hold, contest, eliminate, retreat, or protect a lead until time expires?

Do not assume fixed timing

Phases are states, not a clock.

A battle can move from opening to crisis quickly. A late capture swing can make a previously quiet lane decisive. Use events and scoring state—not rigid minute marks—to identify the current phase.

Update after each major event

Recalculate the map.

When a destroyer is lost, a flank retreats, an objective changes hands, or an enemy group is spotted, the support network and likely routes change. Reassess rather than extend an outdated plan.

32 · Decision branches

Build the response around the enemy's next choice.

Enemy advances

Can the team hold without becoming isolated?

If yes, keep support connected and punish the advance where firing lines permit. If no, withdraw in an orderly way and look for a safer point to re-engage.

Enemy retreats

Is pursuit better than control?

Do not chase automatically. Preserve the objective, maintain spotting if safe, and exploit the space the enemy has yielded.

Enemy concentrates

Can the opposite side be exploited?

Estimate whether the concentrated group can break through before a rotation pays off. Avoid sacrificing a flank merely to make a theoretical counter-push.

Enemy disappears

What routes remain plausible?

Keep more than one hypothesis alive. Guard likely passages and use fresh detection to narrow the search area.

33 · Competitive context

Competitive teams can execute a plan. The map still decides whether it works.

Individually matched battles

Coordination is uncertain

  • Ships may choose lanes independently.
  • Support may arrive late or not at all.
  • Predictions should account for inconsistent commitment and opportunistic movement.
  • A simple, robust plan can outperform a complex plan that requires perfect cooperation.
Organized competitive teams

Coordination can be deliberate

  • Teams can assign scouting, support, and crossfire roles before the battle.
  • Concentration and rotation can be timed across several ships.
  • Captains can trade one area for another as part of an agreed plan.
  • Enemy movement may be coordinated rather than a series of isolated decisions.

A map supports a hypothesis, not certainty.

A static image shows geometry and visible positions. It cannot establish every condition needed to know what will happen next.

Unknown from a static map

Conditions a map outline cannot prove
